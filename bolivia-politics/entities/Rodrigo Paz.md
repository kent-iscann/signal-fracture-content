# Rodrigo Paz

President of Bolivia since November 2025. Son of former president Jaime Paz Zamora and grandson of former president Víctor Paz Estenssoro. Born in Spain to a Spanish mother. Described as coming from the "caviar left" — combining his father's Revolutionary Left Movement (MIR) heritage with substantial education and professional experience in the U.S. financial sector.

Campaigned as centrist but governed with right-wing coalition after entering office. Described by CSIS as an "accidental president" whose contemplative, intellectual tradition has inhibited decisive action in managing the crisis.

Key decisions: eliminated fuel subsidies (triggering inflation — gasoline up 86%, diesel up 160%), struck deals with right-wing parties in Congress, excluded populist VP from cabinet. Backed by the Trump administration as part of a broader strategy to increase U.S. influence in the hemisphere.

Facing existential crisis after 50+ days of protests demanding resignation. Signed agreement with Bolivian Workers' Confederation (COB) on June 19, 2026 as step toward resolution. Declared state of emergency June 20, 2026. CSIS assessment (June 30): will likely survive current struggle but could be forced to resign before end of 2026.

Has warned Evo Morales that "jail is coming," referencing Morales's arrest warrant for aggravated human trafficking. In a June 18, 2026 CNN interview, Paz hinted Morales arrest could come "maybe this weekend" or by end of month, and described Morales's support base as "a sect." Paz's government is also seeking U.S. judicial cooperation to investigate Morales's alleged drug trafficking links.

On June 26, 2026, Paz's government ended the 15-year dollar peg, devaluing the boliviano by ~30% (6.96 to 9.73 per dollar) and transitioning to a floating exchange rate. Economy Minister José Gabriel Espinoza announced an IMF financing programme advancing toward $5 billion. Paz received formal US backing for his state of emergency from the Trump administration. Moody's (June 30) maintained Bolivia's Caa3 rating with projected 2026 economic contraction but credited Paz's willingness to reform.

Paz's fuel subsidy elimination (up to 100% price increases for gasoline/diesel) included a novel redistribution mechanism: 50% of savings transferred to regional/subnational governments for hospitals, schools, and public services. Paz described inheriting "a country wounded in its economy, without dollars, with rising inflation and without fuel."

In early June 2026, the Trump administration threw its full weight behind Paz. Secretary of State Marco Rubio assured Paz of "ramping up emergency assistance and logistics operations support" to address acute food and medical shortages caused by roadblocks. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth denounced "attempts to overthrow the legitimate government" and pledged continued support against "narco-terrorists." Hegseth's framing of protesters as "narco-terrorists profiting from death and destruction in our hemisphere" aligned with Paz's own government narrative linking Morales-linked groups to drug trafficking.

|In late July 2026, the IMF reached a staff-level agreement with Bolivia on a $1.9 billion Extended Fund Facility — well below earlier $5 billion expectations. The same week, prosecutors issued a second arrest warrant for Evo Morales on charges of armed insurrection and terrorism over the blockades. Diesel shortages threatened Santa Cruz's $1B+ soybean harvest, compounding the economic crisis. Paz survived the May–June protest wave but faced compounded governance challenges entering August 2026, with his probability of surviving through end of 2026 adjusted to 60% (−5pp).

|By mid-July 2026, the Paz administration's security crackdown had intensified: a $20M US agreement to train and arm Bolivian security forces was signed; the DEA opened a new La Paz office (May); authorities arrested peasant leaders David Mamani and David Quispe Machaca on terrorism charges (July 17); 583 total arrests were recorded at the protest height; 114 criminal cases opened (75 ex officio); 335 under investigation. The IACHR denounced police excessive use of force coordinated with right-wing vigilante groups. Hegseth labeled all demonstrators "narcoterrorists." Milei and Kast sent "humanitarian aid" to Bolivia later investigated for including repressive material. The government signaled it would not lift the 90-day state of exception until "no longer needed," drawing comparisons from human rights lawyers to the 2019 pre-massacre period.

In July 2026, Paz's government took a significant step by authorizing private importation of fuel, partially breaking the YPFB state monopoly to alleviate acute gasoline and diesel shortages. Bolivia also joined the Shield of the Americas (Escudo de las Américas), a regional military-security alliance, as part of Paz's reestablishment of close relations with the United States.

By August 2026, Paz's political position showed new vulnerabilities. Reuters (Aug 11) reported his center-right coalition was showing signs of unraveling — Congress was fragmented among six center-right blocs with uncertain priorities, tensions emerged within the ruling Christian Democratic Party (PDC) between Paz and Vice President Edmand Lara, and several former allies had left the cabinet. Political analyst Andrés Gómez (Universidad Mayor de San Andrés) assessed that "building a coalition is the government's challenge at this moment." Paz's administration proposed a major energy reform package: capping combined tax and royalty burdens on energy companies at ~50% to compete with Peru and Argentina, providing incentives for new exploration, reducing the role of state energy firm YPFB, strengthening legal protections for investors, and allowing greater private-sector participation in electricity generation and trade. The congressional debate on these reforms and the IMF $1.9B program was seen as an early measure of Paz's political strength — with opposition lawmaker Alejandro Reyes (Unity Alliance) demanding "dollar by dollar" transparency.
