# Bolivia's Post-MAS Fragmentation

Bolivia's political landscape after MAS is more fractured than during the party's dominance. The current crisis is not simply left-right polarization — protests against Paz come largely from sectors that once supported him. Subnational election results reflect fragmentation. Governing is harder but power is less concentrated. Morales dominates headlines but is more visible than influential. New political actors and alliances are emerging that don't map onto the old MAS vs. anti-MAS binary.

Key structural factors:
- **De-institutionalization**: Paz's winning coalition "lacked a party structure, ideology, or clear platform" (Phenomenal World, June 2026). This ambiguity explains the government's weakness in the face of unprecedented mobilizations.
- **MAS's internal collapse**: Three-way infighting between Morales, Arce, and Rodríguez destroyed the party apparatus pre-election. The post-MAS vacuum was filled by a heterogeneous centrist coalition without institutional roots.
- **Clientelist legacy**: MAS's 20-year system of corporate mediation between state and social movements (Indigenous organizations, peasant unions) hollowed out institutionalized political representation. Opposition parties were systematically marginalized.
- **Economic rupture**: The IMF "prior actions" dynamic — austerity measures implemented secretly before any loan agreement is public — mirrors the neoliberal shock therapy of the 1980s-90s that permanently discredited these institutions. Reserves collapsed from $15B to $4.5B; fuel imports now $3B/year vs. $1B in gas exports.

In late June 2026, Paz's government escalated economic reforms: ending the 15-year dollar peg (June 26), devaluing the boliviano by ~30%, and advancing an IMF programme toward $5 billion (June 29). June inflation hit 5.2% — the highest monthly rate in 38 years — driven by food, fuel, and import cost pass-through from the devaluation. By late July, the IMF staff-level agreement came in at $1.9 billion — well below expectations — while acute diesel shortages threatened the soybean harvest and the judicial crisis deepened. The currency devaluation and IMF engagement mark a shift from crisis management to structural transformation, but the complementary reforms (investment law, hydrocarbon reform) remain stalled in Congress.

By August 2026, the fragmentation had extended into the government itself. Reuters (Aug 11) reported Congress was divided among six center-right blocs with uncertain priorities, the ruling PDC was split by tensions between Paz and Vice President Edmand Lara, and several former allies had left the cabinet. The proposed energy reform package — capping tax/royalty burdens at ~50%, reducing YPFB's role, opening electricity to private participation — faces intense congressional scrutiny. Meanwhile the poverty data deepened the stakes: the 2025 household survey showed poverty leapt from 38.1% to 44.7% in a single year (5.35M people), with extreme poverty rising from 12.8% to 16.7%, and World Bank/PAHO data found 52% food insecurity. The Paz government's July authorization of private fuel imports partially broke the YPFB state monopoly — a structural shift in the state-dominated economy model. Fragmentation now operates on three levels: a collapsed MAS, a fractured six-bloc legislature, and a coalition government shedding its own allies.

On August 18, 2026, the fragmentation reached a new peak: Congress voted to censure Economy Minister José Gabriel Espinoza, the architect of Paz's economic policy and lead IMF negotiator. The government challenged the constitutional validity of the 91-vote censure (short of 2/3 majority), but the episode exposed the government's inability to command its own coalition. The same day, the arrest of Paz's campaign adviser Fernando Cerimedo for plotting the murder of a critic revealed Decree 5503 — a radical economic reform package including fuel subsidy elimination being prepared outside normal legislative channels. The Cerimedo-Beller scandal and Decree 5503 revelations suggest fragmentation may be accelerating from political division into institutional crisis, with shadow governance structures operating alongside the formal six-bloc Congress.
