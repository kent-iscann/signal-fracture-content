## Analysis

### Political

The Sept 14 election structurally ended the transitional era and opened a coalition-formation scramble as much about national politics as Bangsamoro governance. Neither MILF-descended party can govern alone: Macacua's BFP took 31 seats, Murad Ebrahim's UBJP 24, leaving the 8-seat BGC of entrenched political clans as the decisive kingmaker (COMELEC official proclamation). Macacua — appointed interim Chief Minister by Marcos in March 2025 over MILF objections, stripped of BIAF command alongside 40+ commanders in early September — is Marcos-aligned and explicitly backed the President in 2022, while the MILF backed Leni Robredo. A BFP-led government would extend Malacañang's control over the BARMM executive.

Rappler's analysis flags that the Duterte camp has incentive to back Murad/UBJP against the Marcos-tied Macacua, potentially importing the 2028 national rivalry into the region's first elected parliament. MILF First Vice Chair Iqbal previously called the Macacua installation a "regime change" outside the CAB's bilateral channels, meaning a Macacua chief ministry would consolidate a Manila-sanctioned successor leadership that the MILF's formal organs have repudiated. The practical stakes are immediate — the new parliament convenes October 30 and must inherit unfinished legislative business including the Bangsamoro Revenue Code, which sat deferred in the BTA.

### Economic

The economic lever runs through the ₱114-billion BARMM budget and the deeply unfinished socioeconomic side of normalization. BARMM is overwhelmingly dependent on the national annual block grant, so whichever coalition governs still relies on Malacañang's budget releases. The decommissioning dispute is now data-rich on both sides: the government counters MILF's charge that "not a single one has successfully transitioned to productive civilian life" by citing ₱100,000 cash assistance per decommissioned fighter (26,000+ processed) and ₱4 billion+ in programs since 2019, while admitting ₱788 million earmarked for final decommissioning was returned to the Treasury because of MILF postponements.

Roughly 13,855 combatants and 2,575 weapons remain to be processed. OPAPRU's pivot to a Colombia-style monthly stipend for decommissioned combatants (mostly aged 50-65, for whom vocational training is impractical) signals Manila is reaching for new financial instruments because one-off lump sums have not produced durable civilian livelihoods. The World Bank-managed Bangsamoro Normalization Trust Fund and EU/Japan/Australia camp-transformation projects (₱4 billion in roads, health, water, solar) remain the structural economic antidote to re-radicalization, but their delivery now depends on a coalition government with no built-in majority, raising the risk that socioeconomic delivery slows further amid coalition bargaining.

### Military

The security picture is bifurcated: the state-vs-jihadist threat is at a historic low even as the MILF's internal fracture and firearms proliferation pose the sharper near-term risk. Crisis Group Report #358 ("Policing the Bangsamoro," Sept 3) documents that clan feuds (rido), political rivalries, and criminal networks now pose a greater threat than state-militant fighting; the region holds an estimated 27,000+ unregistered firearms; and the PNP remains weak and politicized such that the military still carries the burden of public order.

ASG has been declared an "inactive group" (JAPIC, April 2026) with membership collapsed from 130 (2021) to ~50. ISEAP lost its "province" designation (Oct 2024–Jan 2026) with attacks near zero and central ISIS funding ceased since 2022 (MEMRI). However, the same assessments flag DIMG remnants recruiting discreetly, two Tajik nationals sighted with DIMG in April 2026, and foreign-fighter transit persisting through BARMM's ungoverned spaces.

The acute risk is the MILF split itself: the Macacua ambush (Aug 15) demonstrated that intra-MILF rivalries have acquired an armed dimension. Crisis Group and other analysts warn that rival blocs and clan elites may enlist armed groups — including ASG and BIFF remnants that AFP-WestMinCom explicitly flagged as exploitable "tools for coercion" during the election — to intimidate or disrupt the coalition process. The AHJAG and CPC ceasefire-mechanism extensions (to 2027 and 2028) and the SALW management framework agreed at the Sept 25 Kuala Lumpur talks are the formal instruments meant to contain this spillover, but they depend on a functioning civilian government that has yet to be formed.

### Technological

Technology's role is concentrated in electoral integrity and firearms control rather than kinetic counterterrorism. The pre-shaded-ballot discovery in 36 Maluso, Basilan precincts — which forced COMELEC to replace ballots and extend voting — and the 800+ teacher withdrawals from election duties point to systemic procedural vulnerabilities in the region's first automated parliamentary exercise, though 84.34% turnout and 108 IRI international observers provided compensating transparency.

On the security-technology front, the proposed GPH-MILF Framework for Small Arms and Light Weapons Management (SALW-M) is the most concrete technological-policy deliverable: it channels decommissioning, private-armed-group disbandment, and firearms reduction into a codified program routed through the Joint Normalization Committee, with ceasefire mechanisms renewed in the same Sept 25 Kuala Lumpur package. Notably absent is any evidence of meaningful state investment in community-level P/CVE surveillance or monitoring technology. The Bangsamoro Revenue Code bill — a fiscal-institutional technology for self-governance — sat deferred in the BTA and now awaits a coalition parliament, making the region's financial apparatus itself hostage to political deadlock.
