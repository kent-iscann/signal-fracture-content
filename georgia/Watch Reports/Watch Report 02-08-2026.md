## What's New

- **China exits Anaklia Deep Sea Port (July 6):** CCCC and China Harbour Investment formally walked away from their 49% stake, ending the flagship project of Georgia's China pivot. GD announced a "landlord" model — state retains core infrastructure, seeking multiple international partners. Economy Minister Kvrivishvili invited China, Central Asia, and Azerbaijan — but not Europe — to participate. 2029 target for first phase. Uzbek officials expressed vague interest. This is the most structurally significant counter-development to the "deepening China ties" thesis.

- **South Ossetia governance absorbed into Russian structures (June 23, reported July 22):** De facto president Alan Gagloev resigned and was appointed Putin adviser. Russian citizen Marat Kambolov (former Kurchatov Institute director) installed as acting president. The 2026 Treaty on Deepening Allied Interaction continued integration — provisions allow Russian citizens to hold posts in South Ossetia's government. MRB Bank documented as a cut-out for Donbas financial intermediation ($49B in 2017) and North Korea sanctions evasion. Georgia's economic dependence on Russia deepened: 97% wheat imports, 60-65% wine exports.

- **Kobakhidze completes Central Asia sweep — all five states (by July 21):** PM met leaders of Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, and Turkmenistan over summer 2026. Strategic partnership agreements signed with Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. First-ever Georgian PM visits to Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan (11 bilateral documents signed). Turkmen President Berdimuhamedov's first official Tbilisi visit. But exports are dominated by vehicle re-exports (95.5% to Kyrgyzstan, ~90% to Tajikistan) — economically trivial. Experts warn Central Asia cannot substitute for Western ties.

Georgia alone in the region is narrowing its strategic agency while Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Central Asian states expand theirs. GD burning Western bridges without cultivating viable alternatives.

TRIPP expected to reduce travel times 25% vs Baku-Tbilisi-Kars. Geography alone cannot compensate for deteriorating political trust.

- **Russian diaspora soft-power strategy exposed (OC Media, July):** Leaked December 2025 Moscow State University report for the Presidential Administration's new soft-power unit. Budget: ₽13B ($165M). Georgia is primary destination for post-2022 Russian emigrants (15.5%). Russian Orthodox Church identified as key channel. Vucic's first head-of-state Tbilisi visit (June); free-trade deal ($20M bilateral trade).

- **Gakharia calls for conditional US engagement (July):** Former PM argues GD's trajectory is "a direct challenge to a continued American stake in the region." ## Analysis

### Political

The most consequential development since the last report is the structural unraveling of GD's China pivot narrative. China's exit from the Anaklia Deep Sea Port — the flagship infrastructure project that anchored the "comprehensive strategic partnership" signed just one month earlier — is more than a contractual setback. It exposes the fundamental asymmetry in GD's alternative-partner strategy: Georgia needs China far more than China needs Georgia. The Diplomat's June analysis proved prescient: the partnership was indeed a "louder signal, an empty promise."

But the strategic drift itself has not slowed. It has simply reconfigured its vectors. The South Ossetia governance handover — Gagloev's resignation, Kambolov's installation, the continued implementation of the 2026 Treaty on Deepening Allied Interaction — represents a qualitatively new phase in Russia's absorption of Georgian territory. This is no longer frozen-conflict management but administrative integration, with a Russian citizen now governing South Ossetia and Russian law progressively replacing local institutions. The GEOpolitics analysis's documentation of MRB Bank's role in Donbas and North Korea sanctions evasion adds a financial-crime dimension that directly implicates Georgia's territory in some of the world's most sanctioned activities.

GD's domestic trajectory compounds the geopolitical drift. The Kelly/Kramer warning (Just Security, July 29) is the most alarming institutional assessment to date: proposed Constitutional Court legislation to ban opposition parties, the creation of a "systemic opposition" modeled on United Russia, and eight opposition leaders facing sabotage charges carrying up to 15 years. The verdict, expected in fall 2026, will be a decisive moment. If the opposition is effectively criminalized, Georgia crosses a threshold from competitive authoritarianism to one-party state. This level of repression has no precedent in Georgia's post-Soviet history and accelerates the country's departure from the European political space.

The US engagement paradox deepened during July. Trump's Independence Day message — "Looking Forward to Strengthening Cooperation" — and Botchorishvili's participation in Rubio's ministerial suggest the executive branch is maintaining the reset channel. But the US Embassy's July 28 rebuke of GD's "Hate Speech" division signals continued discomfort with the domestic trajectory. The absence of a Senate-confirmed ambassador in Tbilisi — flagged by Kelly and Kramer — is a structural indicator that Washington has not normalized the relationship. The reset remains contested, and the executive-congressional divide is the defining feature of US-Georgia policy.

The OSCE PA Hague Declaration (July 8) adds a multilateral dimension to the pressure. Combined with the EU's continued frozen accession, Georgia now faces criticism from virtually every major multilateral institution in the Euro-Atlantic space. The EU Awareness Centre analysis by Ambassador Dondua provides the most sophisticated diagnosis of how Russia exploited this dynamic — using cognitive warfare tactics that the West failed to counter effectively, particularly the "fear of war" narrative that equates EU integration with military conflict.

The contrast with Armenia's westward trajectory continues to sharpen. Pashinyan's identification of frozen EU-Tbilisi dialogue as the "biggest obstacle" to Armenia's EU ambitions (SCEEUS, June 23) makes Georgia's drift not just a bilateral problem but a regional structural constraint. The Caucasus Watch analysis captured this precisely: Georgia's risk is not isolation but "marginalization by design" — being written out of regional architecture as Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Turkey reshape the South Caucasus without Tbilisi's meaningful participation.

### Economic

China's exit from Anaklia is the economic story of the month. The "landlord model" GD announced is untested — no country in the region has successfully built a major deep-water port through this structure without a dominant anchor investor. The invitation list (China, Central Asia, Azerbaijan, but not Europe) signals GD's continued preference for non-Western partners even after the Chinese consortium walked away. This is analytically significant: GD would rather court vague Uzbek interest than open Anaklia to European investment, suggesting the ideological commitment to non-Western alignment now overrides pragmatic infrastructure economics.

The Central Asia pivot, while diplomatically active, remains economically hollow. JAMnews's July 21 analysis is devastating on the data: 95.5% of Georgia's $355M exports to Kyrgyzstan are vehicle re-exports — essentially used cars transiting Georgia rather than Georgian production finding new markets. The same pattern holds for Tajikistan (~90%). Kazakhstan's oil transit via BTC pipeline (49M barrels in Q1 2026) is the sole genuinely strategic economic link, and it predates the current diplomatic push. The Central Asia strategy is geopolitically significant as a signal of intent but economically trivial in terms of genuinely new value creation.

The South Ossetia integration carries economic implications beyond the political. Georgia's documented dependence on Russia — 97% of wheat/flour imports, 60-65% of wine exports — creates structural vulnerability that the South Ossetia administrative absorption deepens. As Russian law progressively replaces local institutions, the occupied territory's economy becomes a permanent appendage of Russia's, and Georgia's leverage over reintegration — always theoretical — becomes even more remote.

The ISPI Middle Corridor paradox (July 27) illuminates the economic contradiction at the heart of GD's strategy. Georgia wants the economic benefits of the Middle Corridor — transit fees, logistics sector growth, strategic relevance — without accepting the political conditions (democratic governance, rule of law, EU alignment) that the corridor's European terminus requires. Economy Minister Kvrivishvili's decision to exclude European partners from Anaklia invitations crystallizes this contradiction. The TRIPP route, expected to reduce travel times 25% vs Baku-Tbilisi-Kars, could structurally reduce Georgia's transit value if it materializes — and Georgia is not at the table shaping it.

The wine industry continues to exemplify Russia's economic leverage. The Forbes analysis from June remains operative: Russia buys 60-65% of exports, and the 2025 excise tax increases caused a 15.8% export drop. The pattern — dependency, coercion, attempted diversification, dependency rebuild — is a microcosm of Georgia's broader geopolitical vulnerability, and nothing in July's data suggests this dynamic has changed.

### Military

No significant changes in hard military posture, but the South Ossetia governance absorption represents the most significant soft-military development. The installation of a Russian citizen (Kambolov, former Kurchatov Institute director — a nuclear and defense research institution) as acting president is not a military occupation in the traditional sense, but it achieves the same result: the progressive elimination of any autonomous governance capacity in the occupied territory. The 2026 Treaty on Deepening Allied Interaction's provisions for coordinated foreign, defense, and security policies formalize what was already de facto — but with the governance shift, the formal and de facto are converging.

MRB Bank's documented role in Donbas financial intermediation ($49B in 2017) and North Korea sanctions evasion (US Treasury sanction, September 2024) adds a military-finance dimension. The occupied territory is not just a Russian military outpost — it is a financial infrastructure for Russia's most sanctioned activities, including those directly related to the Ukraine war.

Russia's occupation of ~20% of Georgian territory remains the defining military fact. GD's continued demilitarization of the Western alignment — no NATO exercises, no military cooperation with Ukraine, no reintegration rhetoric — remains in place. The Zurabishvili framing from June — that GD foreign policy is shaped by "what Russia would tolerate" — remains the most accurate characterization of the military dimension.

### Technological

The Russian diaspora soft-power strategy (OC Media, July) represents a technological dimension of influence that Western analysis often overlooks. The leaked Moscow State University report — described as an updated 1968 KGB manual — reveals a systematic approach to diaspora engagement with a ₽13B ($165M) budget. Georgia's status as the primary destination for post-2022 Russian emigrants (15.5%) makes it the primary target. The recommendations — apolitical Sunday schools, family-oriented Russian House programming, Soviet-era cultural nostalgia — are designed to operate below the threshold of political contestation, making them harder to counter than overt propaganda.

The EU Awareness Centre analysis adds a cognitive warfare dimension. Ambassador Dondua documents three Russian narratives — the Saakashvili factor, fear of war, anti-LGBTQ+ "values war" — that exploited genuine vulnerabilities in Western messaging. The technological sophistication lies not in the tools (traditional media, social platforms, church networks) but in the precision of the targeting: Russia deployed "destructive capital" with greater accuracy than the West's "constructive capital."

The "Hate Speech" Interior Ministry division, rebuked by the US Embassy (July 28), adds a domestic surveillance dimension. Critics argue it will systematically monitor social media and automatically prosecute government critics. If implemented, this would represent a significant expansion of the state's technological capacity for information control — and potentially intersect with Chinese surveillance technology procurement, a concern that HR 7668 explicitly addresses.

Anaklia port's digital infrastructure — port management systems, customs automation, logistics software — remains a concern for Western security planners. The "landlord model" may retain state ownership of physical infrastructure, but the question of who provides the technology layer is unresolved and significant. A port that moves Chinese goods through Georgian territory is one thing; a port whose digital infrastructure is provided by Chinese state-backed firms is another. HR 7668's classified intelligence assessment, if the Senate passes the bill, will presumably examine this dimension.
