## Analysis

### Political

The dominant development since the last report is the dramatic escalation of US-Georgia tensions triggered by the China pivot. The sequence was textbook regime survival doctrine in action: US House passes HR 7668 targeting Chinese influence → Kobakhidze signs comprehensive strategic partnership with China the next day → dismisses US concerns as treating Georgia like "a schoolboy who can be held back."

This is not a clean break. Kobakhidze is still having "specific conversations" with the Trump administration. The State Department delegation visits (two in May) continue. But the reset has become visibly contested — Congress is moving toward conditionality (intelligence assessment, 5-year strategy review) while the executive branch pursues engagement. The National Interest analysis captured the Washington debate: normalizing GD without behavioral change would be "strategic self-sabotage."

The Iran vector adds a qualitatively new dimension. Previous reports focused on Russia, China, and the US as Georgia's primary external interlocutors. Iran's emergence — with 13,000 companies, sanctions evasion infrastructure, and political influence at the highest levels — creates a new friction point that Washington views through a national security lens rather than a democracy-promotion or strategic-competition lens. The Hudson Institute report and Kobakhidze's threatening response toward its co-author signal that GD is willing to defend this vector aggressively.

Zurabishvili's Washington interview (June 16) is the most consequential voice from Georgia's pro-Western camp. Her framing — Georgia as emerging "gray zone," GD foreign policy shaped by "what Russia would tolerate," snap elections possible — provides Washington with a counter-narrative to GD's reset rhetoric. Her receipt of the Freedom House 2026 Mark Palmer Prize amplifies her platform.

The domestic trajectory continues to deteriorate. The new "hate speech" Interior Ministry department formalizes what was previously ad hoc social media suppression. Combined with the April 2025 Foreign Agents Registration Act (criminal penalties up to 5 years prison) and the Broadcasting Law (ban on all foreign funding), GD has constructed a comprehensive architecture of information control. The PRIF analysis documents how GD captured EU transparency norms to build repressive media laws — the EU's formal conditionality has been neutralized.

The contrast with Armenia sharpens weekly. Pashinyan won reelection (49.8%), TRIPP was finalized, the EU launched visa liberalization and an EUPM mission, and Turkey-Armenia direct trade resumed. Armenia is moving westward while Georgia drifts — and the divergence is increasingly visible to both Washington and Brussels.

### Economic

Georgia's economy continues to perform well (10.7% YoY GDP growth in March 2026, 9.1% Q1 average), driven by its transit hub role, ICT, construction, and logistics. This economic momentum gives GD confidence that it can sustain its political trajectory without immediate economic consequences.

The China economic relationship is deepening rapidly: trade grew 45% YoY (Jan-Apr 2026), China is now Georgia's 3rd largest trading partner and 2nd in exports. The comprehensive strategic partnership signed June 9 formalizes what was already accelerating. But The Diplomat's analysis raises an important caution: Anaklia deep-water port — the flagship Chinese infrastructure project — "has yet to leave port." China is not yet a significant driver of Georgia's tourism economy. The partnership may be a louder signal than substantive commitment from Beijing's side.

The Kobakhidze Central Asia pivot (Tajikistan, June 19; Kyrgyzstan, earlier in June) adds an economic dimension to Georgia's diplomatic diversification. Eleven documents signed with Tajikistan. The strategic logic is clear: link the China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan railway with Black Sea routes via Georgia. But this is aspirational — the infrastructure does not yet exist, and Central Asian cargo volumes through Georgia are minimal. The visits signal intent more than immediate economic returns.

The Forbes wine analysis documents a structural economic vulnerability: Russia buys 60-65% of Georgia's wine exports. Russia's 2025 excise tax increases caused a 15.8% export drop (24% decline in Russia-bound shipments). The historical precedent is clear: the 2006 embargo caused a 50% export loss, and dependency was rebuilt to 66% by 2024. The US market is growing (15.5% CAGR) but cannot absorb the Russian shortfall. This pattern — dependency, coercion, diversification attempt, dependency rebuild — is a microcosm of Georgia's broader geopolitical vulnerability.

The Azerbaijan-Türkiye-Georgia trilateral (10th meeting, Istanbul) reinforces Georgia's transit corridor value. The Baku-Tbilisi-Kars railway reopened June 2, restoring daily passenger service. The Turkey-Georgia electricity barter arrangement adds an energy dimension. These economic ties with Turkey and Azerbaijan provide Georgia with non-Western economic anchors that sustain it through political isolation.

The shadow fleet incident (UK-sanctioned Russian vessel at Kulevi Port, February 2026) is a warning sign. If Georgia becomes a sanctions-evasion hub — for Russian shadow fleet vessels, for Iranian companies, for unregulated cryptocurrency — it risks secondary sanctions that would directly impact its economy, regardless of GD's political preferences.

### Military

No significant changes in the military dimension since the last report. Russia's occupation of 20% of Georgian territory remains the defining military fact. GD continues its deliberate demilitarization of the Western alignment — no NATO exercises, no military cooperation with Ukraine, no reintegration rhetoric. Russia's creeping borderization continues in Abkhazia and South Ossetia.

The US reset has not included any visible security component. The NDAA amendments approved by the House Armed Services Committee (on political prisoners and Russian/Chinese intelligence operations) suggest that Congress, not the executive branch, is driving the security dimension of US-Georgia policy.

The military balance heavily favors Russia, and GD's caution is driven by the fear of becoming "a second Ukraine." Zurabishvili's analysis — that GD foreign policy is shaped by "what Russia would tolerate" — frames this as deference rather than deterrence. The distinction matters: if GD is deferring to Russian preferences, its room for maneuver is narrower than if it were pursuing an independent course.

### Technological

The ICT sector continues to grow as part of Georgia's economic success. But the technological dimension of Georgia's geopolitical positioning is increasingly dominated by US-China competition concerns. Chinese tech investment (potential 5G infrastructure, surveillance systems, port digital infrastructure) is a concern for Washington that HR 7668 explicitly addresses. The new "hate speech" Interior Ministry department — which critics argue will monitor social media — adds a domestic surveillance dimension that may intersect with Chinese technology procurement.

The shadow fleet and sanctions-evasion concerns also have a technological dimension: unregulated cryptocurrency activity in Georgia, as flagged by Zurabishvili, represents a regulatory gap that could attract both legitimate fintech and illicit finance. The more Georgia becomes a "gray zone" for financial oversight, the harder it becomes for Western institutions to engage constructively.
