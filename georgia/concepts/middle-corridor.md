# The Middle Corridor

The Middle Corridor (also called the Trans-Caspian International Transport Route) is a multimodal transport route connecting China to Europe via Central Asia, the Caspian Sea, the South Caucasus (Georgia, Azerbaijan), and Turkey — bypassing both Russia and Iran. It has become a central element of Georgia's geopolitical value proposition.

## Route
China → Kazakhstan → Caspian Sea → Azerbaijan → Georgia → Turkey → Europe

## Strategic significance for Georgia
- **Economic:** Transit fees, logistics sector growth, FDI in port/rail infrastructure
- **Geopolitical:** Gives Georgia leverage with both West (alternative to Russian/Iranian routes) and China (land bridge to Europe)
- **US interest:** The Trump administration has emphasized the Middle Corridor as a supply chain diversification priority, partly explaining the US reset with Georgia

## Challenges
- **Capacity:** Still far below the Northern Corridor (through Russia) in volume
- **Bottlenecks:** Caspian Sea crossing, Georgian rail capacity, Turkish border delays
- **Competition:** Armenia-Azerbaijan rapprochement could create alternative routes that bypass Georgia
- **Iran factor:** Disruption of Strait of Hormuz (2026) has increased the Middle Corridor's strategic value

## Key infrastructure
- Baku–Tbilisi–Kars railway (reopened June 2026)
- Batumi and Poti ports
- East-West highway (EU-funded)
- Anaklia Deep Sea Port (planned, Chinese-financed — controversial; Chinese consortium selected over US company, cited in HR 7668 as national security concern)

## 2026 developments
- **Central Asia outreach (June 2026):** Kobakhidze visits Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan — part of Georgia's effort to link Central Asian transport projects (including China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan railway) with Black Sea routes. 11 documents signed with Tajikistan including Joint Intergovernmental Commission. Georgia actively courting Central Asian cargo before corridor maps are finalized.
- Container traffic grew 71% in January-August 2025
- Cargo volume in 2024 was 15x higher than 2023
- Turkey-Armenia trade resumption (June 2026) could create alternative routes bypassing Georgia
- EU weighing investment in Nakhchivan railway project as part of TCTC (contingent on EBRD feasibility study)
- EU committed €500M under Caucasus Transmission Network to synchronize Armenia-Georgia power systems

## July 2026 developments
|- **China exits Anaklia (July 6):** CCCC and China Harbour Investment formally walked away from 49% stake. GD announced "landlord" model — state retains core infrastructure, seeks multiple international partners. 2029 target for first phase.
|- **ISPI Middle Corridor Paradox (July 27):** EU and Georgia have diverging perceptions of the corridor. Economy Minister Kvrivishvili invited China, Central Asia, and Azerbaijan — notably excluding Europe — for Anaklia development. TRIPP expected to reduce travel times 25% vs Baku-Tbilisi-Kars. Geography alone cannot compensate for deteriorating political trust. Corridor participants paradoxically share interest in restoring EU-Georgia relations.

## October 2026 developments
|- **13th Azerbaijan-Turkey-Georgia defense ministerial (Oct 2, Shusha):** The trilateral defense ministers signed a joint protocol, with Turkey's Güler stating "the security of the Caucasus cannot be considered separately from Türkiye, Azerbaijan, and Georgia." Joint exercises Eternity and Caucasus Eagle reaffirmed. The security dimension of the Middle Corridor is increasingly institutionalized alongside the economic/transport dimension.
|- **"Putin's Path" project unveiled (Oct 1):** Russian pro-Kremlin research institutions presented a rail/road transit project linking Russia-Georgia-Armenia via Abkhazia, framed as competing with the Middle Corridor by reasserting Russian-controlled connectivity. Legally unfeasible without Georgian consent but reveals Russia's ambition to undermine Middle Corridor independence.
|- **Bottleneck strategy analysis (Sep 22):** GEOpolitics "Choke, Don't Block" analysis argues Russia's strategy is not to block the Middle Corridor but to prevent it from becoming fully independent of Russian leverage. Uses Georgia's gas transit (Gazprom Armenia) and other infrastructure nodes as examples of "chokepoint sovereignty." Strategic response must multiply and diversify connectivity rather than eliminate it.
