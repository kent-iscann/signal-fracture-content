## Analysis
### Political

The resource nationalism axis has solidified from a Zimbabwe-led experiment into a continental norm with binding consequences. Namibia's July state visit to Beijing produced commitments to domestic processing across uranium, lithium, and rare earths — not as a concession extracted by an assertive host, but as a jointly stated objective embedded in eight cooperation agreements. This marks a shift from adversarial negotiation (Zimbabwe's raw export ban forcing Chinese firms' hands) to pre-emptive integration (Namibia securing processing commitments at the deal-formation stage). Zimbabwe's extension of the raw export ban to 13 additional minerals — cobalt, PGMs, rare earths — signals that the model is being institutionalised rather than treated as a lithium-specific exception. The Quad's $20B Critical Minerals Initiative, launched with unprecedented rhetorical sharpness toward Beijing, represents the most concerted Western countermeasure yet — but its impact depends on disbursement velocity, which the Lobito Corridor experience (ECDPM analysis showing actual Western spend far below headline figures) suggests will be slow. China is counter-positioning through BRICS strategic minerals coordination and through the transboundary DRC-Zambia battery SEZ, which would embed Chinese processing technology in the continent's most ambitious downstream industrial project.

### Economic

The economic evidence supporting the core prediction has strengthened substantially. The Sandawana $300M deal, the Huayou carbonate plant nearing completion, and the Luanshya copper mine restart collectively demonstrate that Chinese firms are not merely pledging processing investments — they are commissioning them. Zimbabwe's beneficiation revenue data (79% value increase on 27% volume growth in Q1 2026) has created a powerful demonstration effect that Namibia, Tanzania, and DRC are now emulating. However, the financing architecture underpinning these investments is shifting in ways that introduce fragility. Chinese sovereign lending — the engine of BRI-era infrastructure — is at a two-decade low. The CGD documents infrastructure falling to less than 25% of China's overseas lending portfolio, replaced by $71.5B in private fossil fuel extraction deals. PPPs, which Beijing has touted as its new preferred vehicle, have failed to gain traction in Africa. This means the processing plants currently being built are financed by private Chinese firms (Huayou, Tsingshan, CNMC) deploying their own balance sheets — a more commercially disciplined model but one that lacks the strategic patience and below-market financing that state-backed BRI lending provided. The Reuters July 31 analysis captures the tension: Chinese investment in Africa surged 114% in 2025, but it is overwhelmingly mineral-extractive, with analysts describing a relationship "late-19th century Britain would recognize." Meanwhile, Angola's $900M Barra do Dande port concession and Zimbabwe's new lithium rail route to Maputo demonstrate that Chinese logistics infrastructure is expanding even as sovereign lending contracts — shifting to privately financed, operationally controlled models that may prove more durable than state-backed loans.

### Military

The strategic minerals competition has escalated from supply chain diversification to explicit military-supply-chain framing. The Quad's $20B framework explicitly links critical minerals to defence industrial bases — the minerals essential for EV batteries are the same minerals essential for advanced weapons systems, radar, and military communications. China's sulphuric acid export curbs in May 2026, documented by the IEA, demonstrate a willingness to use midstream processing inputs as leverage — acid is essential for copper, lithium, cobalt, nickel, and rare earth processing, and China controls the dominant share of global supply. This adds a new dimension to China's technological lock-in: it extends beyond processing plants and port software into the chemical inputs without which mineral processing itself cannot function. The Glencore sale of 40% stakes in DRC copper-cobalt assets to a US-Gulf consortium ($8-9B) represents the most significant Western counter-acquisition in the DRC, but it leaves Glencore with operational control — the consortium secured ownership but not the processing infrastructure or technical expertise that China has embedded through direct mine-to-smelter integration. The US-backed Madagascar rare earths project extends the competition geography beyond the traditional Copperbelt into new mineral frontiers.

### Technological

China's technological lock-in is deepening across four reinforcing vectors. First, proprietary processing technology is now operational at scale: Huayou's lithium carbonate plant, the Kamoa-Kakula copper smelter, and soon the Sandawana processing facility all run on Chinese industrial technology that host nations cannot independently operate or maintain. Second, China's midstream chemical dominance — the IEA documents that outside China, only a handful of suppliers provide battery-grade graphite processing equipment, rare earth magnet production equipment costs are reportedly 10x higher, and lead times are far longer — creates a structural dependency that extends beyond individual plants into the enabling inputs for all mineral processing. Third, the DRC-Zambia transboundary battery SEZ represents an attempt to embed Chinese processing technology at the regional industrial policy level, creating a platform that would shape standards, supply chains, and technical training for a generation. Fourth, the shift from sovereign lending to private Chinese firm investment may paradoxically deepen technological lock-in: private firms deploying their own capital are more motivated to protect proprietary technology as a competitive advantage, whereas state-backed BRI projects were more likely to include technology transfer provisions as part of diplomatic package deals. The beneficiation shift demanded by African governments continues to accelerate technological dependence — local plants operate with Chinese technology, Chinese chemical inputs, and Chinese supply chains for spare parts and consumables.
