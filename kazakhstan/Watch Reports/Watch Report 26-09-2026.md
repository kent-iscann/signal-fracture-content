## Signal & Fracture
**Signal:** Kazakhstan's economic diversification is accelerating across manufacturing, technology, and new export corridors, even as its Russian oil-export lifeline remains under kinetic threat.
**Fracture:** Each counterweight Kazakhstan cultivates binds it deeper into one great-power supply chain, leaving no neutral ground on which a genuinely autonomous economy can stand.

## Analysis

### Political

The defining political fact since August is completion of the 'New Kazakhstan' constitutional architecture. The August 23 Kurultai elections — the first under the new constitution that took effect July 1 — delivered Adilet (into which the long-ruling Amanat merged) a decisive supermajority of 71.17% of the vote and 110 of 145 seats. The March 15 referendum that authorized this consolidation passed with 87.15% approval. The bicameral Mazhilis-Senate structure has been collapsed into a single unicameral Kurultai elected entirely by party list, alongside a new vice presidency and the presidentially appointed, 126-member Khalyk Kenesi (People's Council), which held its first session on September 23-24 and elected former Mazhilis chair Yerlan Koshanov to lead it.

The practical significance is twofold. First, Tokayev has centralized policy execution precisely when the economic agenda demands speed: a supermajority Kurultai and a hand-picked consultative council remove the legislative friction that might otherwise slow the investment-driven diversification program. Second, this consolidation means Kazakhstan's 'multi-vector' posture is now unambiguously a presidential project. The August 14 revision of the Foreign Policy Concept — stripping out Nazarbayev-era framing, adapting to the new constitution, formalizing the Astana International Forum, and adding explicit commitments to protect citizens and businesses abroad while countering disinformation — institutionalizes multi-vectorism under Tokayev's personal brand rather than as a shared national inheritance.

That posture is being rewarded and tested simultaneously. Tokayev's framing of Kazakhstan as a 'middle power' that will derive influence 'from connecting, not choosing' — articulated at the September SCO summit in Bishkek, where he also proposed an International Water Organisation, an SCO transport strategy to 2035, and offered to host the SCO Development Bank — captures the strategy in a sentence. The invitation to the US-hosted G20 summit in Miami (December 14-15) is the clearest payoff: the first time a Kazakh leader has been invited, backed by H1 2026 US trade exceeding $1 billion (+10%) and cumulative US investment above $60 billion.

But the sharpest stress test is the US-China AI dilemma. Reports in August indicated Washington is preparing an ultimatum forcing partners to choose between the American 'Pax Silica' and China's WAICO — the World AI Cooperation Organization that Kazakhstan co-founded. Kazakhstan signed both. Analysts such as Justin Burke have called the 'us-or-them' framing a 'lose-lose' proposition for both Washington and Central Asia, while The National Interest has argued the US should compete rather than demand exclusivity. For Kazakhstan, this is no longer an abstract governance question: it is the template for how every future great-power demand will be structured, and it exposes the core weakness of a strategy built on being indispensable to everyone simultaneously.

### Economic

The headline economic development is conceptual as much as numerical: at the September 25 Kazakhstan-China Investment Forum in Almaty, Tokayev explicitly called for a shift from individual investment projects to 'integrated production ecosystems,' naming transport, industry, critical minerals, digitalization, and agriculture as priority fields. The forum produced 50 commercial agreements worth $8.2 billion, building on the July Shanghai package. China's cumulative investment in Kazakhstan now exceeds $29 billion, more than 9,000 Chinese-participation enterprises operate in the country, and bilateral trade reached $32.4 billion in January-July (+24.1%), on a trajectory toward a $100 billion target. The character of the deals — an electrolytic-aluminum plant with accompanying wind power, a $440 million UBTech robotics localization venture, a $350 million Fufeng corn deep-processing park scaling to $1.15 billion, and dim-sum bond issuance collaborations — signals a genuine effort to move past raw-materials exchange toward manufacturing and technology transfer.

This is the correct direction. It is also, more than ever, integration into a single great power's industrial orbit. Chinese firms now control roughly a quarter of national oil production, and more than half of China-Europe overland freight transits Kazakhstan. The CES Intelligence 'Landbridge Trap' framework — that multi-alignment is a trap, not a hedge, because geography and infrastructure make Kazakhstan simultaneously indispensable and uncontrollable — is gaining empirical support with every new integrated-production memorandum. The diversification that is real is diversification within the Chinese sphere, not independence from it.

The non-China counterweights are real but smaller and slower. South Korea's $19.19 billion, 82-document package is the most significant non-Chinese commitment on record, elevated to a 'future-oriented comprehensive strategic partnership' with a proposed joint critical-minerals company and a nuclear-energy memorandum. Yet the DKNews analysis is blunt: the figure is roughly six times annual bilateral trade, and commercial agreements of this kind differ sharply in their degree of commitment and implementation schedule. The $19 billion will not enter the economy this year or next; it is a pipeline of potential final investment decisions, many years from concrete deployment. The Middle Corridor is the strongest physical counterweight — container traffic up 58% to ~44,000 TEUs, East-West transit up 55%, EU signaling up to €12 billion — but it carries a fraction of CPC oil volumes and moves goods, not the hydrocarbon revenue that still funds the state.

The structural milestone — manufacturing exceeding mining in industrial output for the first time (47.1% vs 46.1% in H1 2026) — must be read against its context. Oil output fell roughly 9% in H1 2026, costing about $3.5 billion, even as oil remained 46.5% of exports and every disruption hit the National Fund, the budget, and the investment pipeline. The macro-stability indicators that anchor the diversification narrative — inflation down to 9.8%, the base rate trimmed to 16.25%, ADB holding 2026 growth at 4.8%, National Fund plus reserves at $127.3 billion, the tenge the region's best performer at +10% — are real, but they are defensive achievements that describe resilience, not a new growth engine. Meanwhile, the supplementary Gazprom agreement (about 11 bcm for 2026, up from ~4 bcm, with a further 9 bcm discussed for 2027) deepens Russian energy dependence precisely when Washington is legislating secondary sanctions against Russian-energy purchasers. Kazakhstan is, in effect, running faster in three directions at once.

### Military

The military dimension has not been the focus of the past two months, but it frames the economic question more than headline activity suggests. Kazakhstan's diplomacy secured a Ukrainian pledge to refrain from further strikes on CPC infrastructure — a meaningful de-escalation after the repeated tanker attacks of July. But the underlying vulnerability is structural and unchanged: the CPC terminal is Russian sovereign territory where Kazakhstan has no military presence, and the Times of Central Asia has documented that attacks shifted from inland pumping stations to offshore loading equipment and civilian tankers. Kazakhstan cannot defend the chokepoint that carries roughly 80% of its crude exports; it can only lobby Kyiv and plead for restraint, which is a geopolitical position, not a security guarantee.

The security consequence is that energy infrastructure has become the economy's single point of failure, and every hedge against it is a physical-infrastructure bet, not a diplomatic one. The expansion of BTC volumes, the Baku-Supsa option, the Kazakhstan-China pipeline (13.1 million tons, with a $327 million expansion approved in September), and the first-ever transit of oil to Kyrgyzstan are all attempts to diversify the physical routing of a vulnerable resource. None of them can replace CPC, but together they begin to reduce the worst-case tail risk.

The nuclear program sharpens the same dynamic in a different register. The $16.5 billion Rosatom deal to build the first nuclear plant at Ulken — construction from 2027, first reactor around 2034 — paired with CNNC's selection for the second and third plants, locks Kazakhstan into Russian and Chinese supply chains for the next four decades. The CSIS Nuclear Network assessment is pointed: the United States never even competed, and 'partnership without infrastructure options is hollow.' For a country framing nuclear power as 'a guarantee of energy sovereignty' and the foundation of its energy-intensive digital economy, the irony is material — the sovereignty project itself deepens dependence on the two powers Kazakhstan most wants to balance against.

Conventional security continues in a defensive register. Kazakhstan launched its fall military draft in early September (about 23,000 conscripts), and Tokayev tasked Security Council staff on 'modern threats' in late September, while the CSTO chief of staff described instability zones being pushed toward the organization's area of operations. None of this represents a near-term kinetic threat to Kazakhstan proper. The real military-relevant risk remains spillover: a re-escalation of strikes on Black Sea energy infrastructure, or a great-power rupture over the AI ultimatum, would transmit directly into Kazakh export revenue without requiring a single shot on Kazakh territory.

### Technological

Technology is the arena where Kazakhstan's diversification ambitions are most advanced and most visibly straining against physical limits. The flagship is Data Center Valley in Ekibastuz, developed with Firebird AI, NVIDIA, and Kazakhtelecom under a $10 billion package targeting 100,000 GPUs including NVIDIA's GB300 and Vera Rubin. Construction is real: excavation for the energy centers and AI factories is 90% complete, a 215 MW substation is acquired, and the first 50 MW facility is scheduled for commissioning in June 2027, scaling toward 1 GW. This is the most concrete step yet toward Tokayev's stated goal of 'transforming Ekibastuz coal into digital export revenue.'

The constraint is electricity, not ambition. Orda.kz's analysis cut through the enthusiasm: a single 300 MW data center would consume roughly 2.1% of all electricity Kazakhstan generated in 2025, and the country does not expect a stable power surplus until 2029. Kazakhstan faced monthly electricity deficits of 125-330 million kWh during the 2025-26 heating season, importing from Russia and Uzbekistan. The nuclear program is, in this sense, downstream of the AI program — computing ambitions are driving energy-transition decisions — but the first reactor will not deliver power until after the first data centers come online. This is a coordination risk of the kind that has repeatedly stalled major Kazakh infrastructure.

The digital-finance and crypto dimension has matured rapidly and represents the clearest hedge against Western financial-system dependence. The comprehensive digital-asset regime that took effect May 1, 2026 created a licensed framework for stablecoins, tokenized real assets, and digital financial assets; the regulated crypto market reached $10.58 billion (from $320 million in 2023), users grew from 53,000 to 215,000, and the national crypto reserve hit $700 million on the way to a $500 million-$1 billion target, alongside a $60 million tokenization pilot for Alatau City. A National Cryptocurrency Analytics Center now monitors crypto-fiat flows. This is a parallel financial infrastructure constructed deliberately to operate alongside, and separately from, the Western system — a sanctions hedge that is also an integration play.

The human-capital and robotics developments complete the picture. Kazakhstan climbed from 122nd to 83rd in the Global Talent Competitiveness Index, the UBTech $440 million joint venture commits to localizing humanoid-and-educational-robot R&D rather than mere assembly, and the Digital Qazaqstan Strategy 2029 targets 80% AI-skilled graduates by 2029. These are genuine, incremental gains. But they share a horizon problem with every other diversification vector: the near-term payoff is measured in 2027-2030, while the structural dependence the prediction tracks is being re-tightened now. An education pipeline, a not-yet-electrified data-centre campus, and a regulated crypto market do not substitute for the export revenue of a disabled CPC or a heavily sanctioned Russian gas trade within an 18-month window.
